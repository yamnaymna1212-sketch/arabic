What happens if you just stop paying your credit card? Not one payment. All of them. Does the bank call your boss? Can they take money straight out of your paycheck? And if they sue you, can you actually go to jail? Today, we're following one credit card all the way to the courtroom. Step by step. And this time, you'll see both endings.

Meet Tyler again. If you saw our last episode, you know Tyler lost his truck. After the repo, he did what millions of Americans do. He started putting everything on his credit card. Groceries. Gas money for rides to work. A phone bill. His balance hits six thousand eight hundred dollars, at twenty-four percent interest. His minimum payment is about two hundred dollars a month. And one month, Tyler just can't make it.

Day one. Honestly? Almost nothing happens. Most cards give you until the due date, and then a late fee lands, often up to around thirty to forty dollars. Interest keeps running on the whole balance. Tyler gets an email and an app notification. If he pays the minimum now, most of this goes away. But Tyler doesn't pay.

Day thirty. This is the first real hit. Once a payment is thirty days late, the bank can report it to the three credit bureaus. And remember, Tyler's score was already bruised from the repo. Now it drops again. Here's the detail most people miss. Once you're sixty days late, many card agreements let the bank switch you to a penalty interest rate, sometimes close to thirty percent. So the balance isn't just sitting there. It's growing faster.

Day sixty to day one-eighty. The calls start. Morning, lunch, evening. Letters stack up on the counter. Tyler turns his phone face down and stops opening the mail. Every month, another late fee. Another chunk of interest. His six thousand eight hundred dollars quietly becomes almost eight thousand.

Then, around day one-eighty, something strange happens. The bank gives up. It's called a charge-off. The bank writes the debt off as a loss on its own books. Tyler thinks, that's it, they forgot about me. They didn't. A charge-off doesn't erase anything. It usually means the debt is about to get a new owner.

Here's something most people don't know. Banks often sell old card debt to companies called debt buyers. And they buy it cheap. A federal study by the FTC found debt buyers paid, on average, about four cents on the dollar. So someone may have paid around three hundred dollars for Tyler's account. And now they want the full eight thousand.

But the new owner has rules too. Under federal law, a debt collector has to send Tyler a validation notice: who they are, how much they say he owes, and who the original creditor was. And Tyler gets a window, usually about thirty days, to dispute it in writing. If he does, the collector has to pause and verify the debt before it keeps collecting. Tyler doesn't send anything. He doesn't even open the envelope.

So it's over, right? The calls stop for a while. Not even close.

Month ten. One afternoon, someone knocks on Tyler's door. A man in a jacket hands him an envelope and says, you've been served. It's a summons. The debt buyer is suing Tyler for eight thousand dollars, plus court costs and attorney fees. Now the total on paper is almost nine thousand four hundred dollars. And at the top of the page, there's a deadline. In many states, Tyler has somewhere between two weeks and a month to file a written answer with the court.

This is the moment that decides everything. Because here's the number that shocked us. Researchers at Pew found that in debt collection lawsuits, more than seventy percent of cases end in a default judgment. That means the person never responded, so the company wins automatically, without proving much of anything.

So let's split the story in two.

Path one. Tyler ignores the summons, just like he ignored everything else. The deadline passes. The judge signs a default judgment. And a judgment is not just a letter. It's a court order. It gives the debt buyer real tools.

Tool number one: wage garnishment. In most states, the creditor can ask Tyler's employer to take money straight from his paycheck. Federal law caps it at twenty-five percent of his disposable earnings, and a few states, like Texas and Pennsylvania, block most wage garnishment for credit card debt entirely. Tool number two: a bank levy. The court can freeze Tyler's checking account and take money from it. One day his card is just declined at the store. On path one, Tyler pays the full nine thousand four hundred, plus interest, one paycheck at a time.

But this time, Tyler doesn't take path one.

Path two. Tyler reads the summons. All of it. He calls a local legal aid office, which helps people with low income for free or at low cost. And he learns three things.

First, he files an answer before the deadline. Just showing up changes the game. Second, he asks the debt buyer to prove it. Prove they actually own his account. Prove the amount. Show the original contract and the paper trail from the bank to them. Debt buyers often buy accounts in huge batches, and sometimes the paperwork is thin. Third, he checks the statute of limitations, the time limit to sue over a debt. In most states it's somewhere between three and six years. Tyler's debt is recent, so that won't save him. But for older debts, it's often the strongest defense there is.

Two months later, the debt buyer's lawyer calls. They don't want to go to trial. They offer a settlement. Tyler agrees to pay three thousand five hundred dollars in monthly payments, and the case is dismissed.

So it's a happy ending, right? Almost. There's one more twist almost nobody sees coming.

In January, Tyler opens his mail and finds a tax form. A 1099-C. Cancellation of debt. Because the debt buyer forgave the rest of his balance, the IRS can treat the forgiven part as income. That's almost six thousand dollars of extra income on paper. Unless he qualifies for an exception, like being insolvent, meaning he owed more than everything he owned, Tyler may owe tax on money he never actually received.

Now let's do the math. Tyler started with six thousand eight hundred dollars on a card. Fees and interest pushed it to eight thousand. The lawsuit pushed it to nine thousand four hundred. On path one, he pays every dollar of that. On path two, he pays three thousand five hundred, plus possibly some tax. Same debt. Same Tyler. The only difference was opening one envelope.

So can Tyler go to jail for credit card debt? No. In the United States, you can't be jailed just for owing money. But there's a catch. If a court orders you to come in and answer questions about your finances and you ignore it, some states can issue a warrant for contempt of court. Not for the debt. For ignoring the judge.

And how long does this follow him? The late payments and the charge-off can stay on Tyler's credit report for up to seven years from when he first fell behind. Here's a small piece of good news: since 2017, the big credit bureaus no longer include civil judgments on credit reports. But a judgment can still be a public record for years, and in many states it can be renewed.

Let's rewind and watch the whole thing. Day one, a late fee. Day thirty, his score drops. Day sixty, a penalty rate. Day one-eighty, a charge-off, and the debt is sold for pennies. Month ten, a knock on the door. And then, a fork. One path ends in garnishment. The other ends in a settlement. All from one month when the minimum payment didn't fit.

So what can you do instead? The earlier you act, the more options you have. Call your card issuer before you miss a payment and ask about a hardship program. Many offer lower rates or smaller payments for a while. A nonprofit credit counselor can set up a debt management plan. If a collector contacts you, ask for validation in writing. And if you're ever served, never ignore it. Read it, mark the deadline, and get help.

Remember, laws are different in every state, and this video is general information, not financial, tax, or legal advice. If you're facing a real lawsuit or a collector, talk to a licensed attorney, a legal aid office, or a nonprofit credit counselor near you.
